Biography

A scholar who never left the street

Wayne Martin Mellinger, Ph.D., is an independent sociologist and public intellectual based in Santa Barbara, California, working at the intersection of ethnomethodology, institutional analysis, and the sociology of homelessness. He trained at the University of California, Santa Barbara under Don H. Zimmerman, Thomas P. Wilson, and Harvey Molotch, in a department that helped found the ethnomethodology and conversation analysis tradition — the close, empirical study of how social order is accomplished in the fine grain of everyday interaction.

For nearly two decades, that training has been tested against the street. As a street outreach worker in Santa Barbara County, Wayne has sat across from people navigating intake interviews, waitlists, discharge planning, and the countless small documentary transactions through which institutions decide who is seen and who is not. That vantage point is the empirical ground for his current theoretical project, Critical Interactionism: an approach that treats abandonment not as an absence of resources or compassion, but as something organizations actively accomplish, in real time, through the ordinary mechanics of paperwork, categorization, and deferral.

He serves on the Santa Barbara County Behavioral Wellness Commission and the Santa Barbara County Continuum of Care, and sits on the boards of several nonprofits working on homelessness and social justice, including CLUE-SB, Showers of Blessing, and the Committee for Social Justice. Since 2012, he has written more than a hundred columns for the online newspaper Noozhawk, translating research and street-level observation for a general audience.

Wayne's scholarship extends into two ongoing research platforms. Doing Modernity: Studies in Critical Interactionism houses his work on institutions, recognition, and the Santa Barbara School of ethnomethodology. The Dionysian Naturalist: Studies in Religious Naturalism and Other Emerging Nature Religions develops a parallel line of inquiry into contemporary Paganism, religious naturalism, and ecstatic spiritual practice — a personal theological framework he calls Dionysian Naturalism.

Each December 21st, Wayne organizes the Longest Night Homeless Memorial Vigil at the Santa Barbara County Courthouse, a ritual of public mourning for community members who died while homeless that year. He co-founded the Santa Barbara Homeless Foot Washing and the Freedom Warming Centers. He is a practicing member of the Unitarian Society of Santa Barbara, and also writes poetry and literary fiction alongside his scholarly and journalistic work.

In 2025, Wayne published I Just Wanted to Dance with Dionysus: Life's Sacred Journey, Lost Souls and Our Thirst for Wholeness, a Gonzo autoethnography tracing his own passage through addiction, homelessness, and psychiatric crisis, framed through Joseph Campbell's Hero's Journey and Dionysian myth. The book turns his own life into data — treating personal experience as a window onto the institutions and social worlds it moved through.

The thread running through all of it: rigorous sociological method, a theology of nature and embodiment, and a refusal to separate scholarship from the work of showing up.

Doing Modernity — The Santa Barbara School: An Annotated Bibliography

Compiled February 2026, ongoing. UCSB has been a major center for ethnomethodology and conversation analysis since the mid-1960s, yet the Santa Barbara School has never been systematically documented as a tradition — this bibliography, and the theoretical framework of Critical Interactionism it develops, is an attempt to remedy that.

I. Overviews & Theoretical Frameworks
Jan 2026The Santa Barbara School of Ethnomethodology and Conversation Analysis

The foundational overview, distinguishing the Santa Barbara School from the UCLA and Boston-Manchester Schools via six defining features — ethnographic integration, structure-in-action, moral order, critical orientation, Goffman engagement, and broad sociological relevance.

Jan 2026From Minds to Moments: How Ethnomethodology and Conversation Analysis Reconceived Sociological Social Psychology

Traces the shift from cognitive and attitudinal models of social psychology to the interactional, sequential approach pioneered at Santa Barbara — an ontological shift, not merely a methodological one.

Jan 2026How UCSB Became a World Center for Studying Race, Policing, and Everyday Interaction

Traces the lineage from Zimmerman through Geoffrey Raymond's and Kevin Whitehead's research on racial categorization in police-civilian encounters, courtroom proceedings, and medical interviews.

Nov 2025The Moral Economy of Interaction

Extends Thompson's and Scott's "moral economy" from markets to meaning, arguing every interaction is a moral event where recognition and respect circulate by norms participants feel as binding. The theoretical cornerstone of Critical Interactionism.

Nov 2025The Layered Moral Economy of Oppression: Completing the Santa Barbara School's Unfinished Revolution

Introduces the Layered Moral Economy of Oppression (LMEO) — oppression operating through six interconnected layers, each reproduced and contested through interactional practice.

Nov 2025The Wilson-Zimmerman Approach to Social Structure

Reconstructs Wilson and Zimmerman's claim that social structure exists nowhere but in the sequential organization of members' practices — inverting the standard relationship between structure and action.

Nov 2025On Maynard and Wilson's "On the Reification of Social Structure" (1980)

A close reading treated as a foundational text for the school's anti-reificationist theory of social structure, connecting mid-century EM/CA debates to contemporary practice theory.

II. Founders & First-Generation Figures
Nov 2025Don H. Zimmerman: The Quiet Maverick of Ethnomethodology

Documents Zimmerman's training under Garfinkel, his 1965 move to UCSB, and five decades as the school's central architect — from bureaucratic sociology through emergency dispatch calls, gender and interaction, and Talk and Social Structure (1991).

Jul 2025Harvey Molotch and the Foundations of Critical Interactionism

Positions Molotch as the school's "critical conscience," analyzing his collaborative work with Lester (news production), Boden (Watergate), and Duneier (urban public space) as demonstrations of interactional detail illuminating power.

Dec 2025When the Doctor is a "Lady": Candace West, the Santa Barbara School, and the Micro-Politics of Gender

Examines West's studies of doctor-patient interaction and "Doing Gender" (1987, with Zimmerman) — arguably the most-cited article in sociology — through to West and Fenstermaker's "Doing Difference" (1995).

Nov 2025Douglas W. Maynard and the Moral Architecture of Institutional Life

Presents Maynard as the school's foremost "moral microsociologist," analyzing his studies of plea bargaining and diagnosis delivery through the perspective-display series and the evidential-interactional nexus.

Nov 2025Democracy Under Threat: Steven Clayman's Interactional Analysis and the Crisis We Can No Longer Ignore

Examines Clayman's study of news interviews and political accountability (including The News Interview, 2002, with John Heritage) as illuminating the interactional machinery of democratic accountability.

Dec 2025Timothy Halkowski and the Mirandum of Ordinary Medical Life

Introduces the "mirandum" — the wonder-worthy features of ordinary medical life patients and practitioners treat as requiring explanation — through Halkowski's studies of medical interaction.

III. Institutional Sites & Applied Work
Nov 2025Organizations Are Talking: Deirdre Boden and the Sequential Constitution of Institutional Order

Examines Boden's The Business of Talk (1994): organizations aren't structures within which talk occurs, but are themselves constituted through the sequential organization of meetings, negotiations, and calls.

Nov 2025On Boden and Molotch's "The Compulsion of Proximity"

On the persistent preference for co-present interaction despite remote communication technology, extended toward questions of who has access to co-presence and who is structurally excluded from it.

Nov 2025On Marilyn Whalen and Jack Whalen's "Studying Workscapes"

On the Whalens' approach to CSCW (Computer-Supported Cooperative Work) research, treating technology as embedded in interactional practice rather than a determinant of behavior.

Oct 2025Ethnomethodology and Conversation Analysis at Xerox PARC: Remaking Technology Through the Social Order

On Lucy Suchman and the Whalens' work at Xerox PARC in the 1980s–90s, studying workplace interaction to redesign office technology — a demonstration of EM/CA's practical applicability.

Feb 2026Jack Whalen and the Documentary Constitution of Organizational Reality

The most recent essay in the series. On how documents — forms, records, databases — actively participate in constituting organizational reality rather than merely recording it.

Nov 2025On Maynard and Zimmerman's "Topical Talk, Ritual and the Social Organization of Relationships"

On how topical talk serves ritual and relational functions beyond information transfer — anticipating the moral economy framework developed in later essays.

Nov 2025Talking City Trouble: Urban Micro-Ethnography and the Moral Order of Public Space

On Molotch and Duneier's study of interaction in urban public space, extended through Wayne's own street outreach experience connecting the literature to homelessness, policing, and urban exclusion.

IV. The Second Santa Barbara School
Dec 2025Jason Turowetz and the Second Santa Barbara School

Profiles Turowetz as representative of the post-2000 generation extending the tradition — his work with Maynard on autism diagnosis, and greater theoretical reflexivity around race, disability, and structural inequality.

V. Critical Interactionism & Normative Questions
Dec 2025What Do We Owe One Another Once We See Asymmetries Being Done

Confronts the normative question hanging over the tradition directly: once gender or racial or class asymmetry is documented with sequential precision, what follows? Develops "ethically engaged interactionism."

Dec 2025Beyond Asymmetries-in-Action: Critical Interactionism, Moral Economy, and the Unequal Distribution of Consequences

Extends the argument above with a theory of how interactional patterns generate differential outcomes that compound across interactions, institutions, and lifetimes.

Religion & Philosophy

Dionysian Naturalism & Critical Drug Studies

Theology under The Dionysian Naturalist, and a parallel platform, Wild Flesh, on drug use and altered states. Reverse-chronological within each category.

The Dionysian Naturalist — Annotated Bibliography (2014–2025)

Compiled by Wayne, February 2026. The essays trace an evolving eco-theological architecture — from early writings on shamanism, recovery, and ecstatic experience, through cosmology and enchanted naturalism, to ritual theory, sacred nature, and political eco-theology.

2014The Ordeal

A poem exploring struggle, transformation, and the sacred journey through difficulty — establishing the spiritual and existential concerns that animate the later theological writings.

2014The Amethyst Path: Shamanism, Dionysian Spirituality and Recovery from "Addiction"

The foundational essay introducing the Amethyst Path — a shamanic approach to addiction recovery drawing on Dionysian spirituality while maintaining sobriety. Critiques biomedical models of addiction through Jungian psychology, shamanic tradition, and the archetype of the wounded healer.

2015Steps Toward a Dionysian Naturalism: Making the Earth Sacred in a Time of Ecological Disaster

Parts 1–3. The programmatic statement establishing Dionysian Naturalism as a distinct theological approach — arguing that de-sacralization of nature is a central cause of ecological crisis, via Nietzsche's Apollonian–Dionysian distinction.

2015Our Universe Is a Sacred Living System

Parts 1–3. Traces the shift from archaic views of nature as sacred and animate to modern mechanistic, disenchanted views, and argues for nature as a "sacred living system." The cosmological foundation of Dionysian Naturalism's worldview.

2015–16Enchanting Naturalism

Parts 1–2. A call for an "enchanted naturalism" integrating scientific evidence with religious inspiration, addressing Weber's disenchantment thesis while rejecting supernaturalism.

2016Dancing with Dionysus: Ecstasy and Religion in the Age of the Anthropocene

Parts 1–4. An autobiographical and theological exploration of "Godless Paganism," tracing the journey from New England Congregationalism through atheism to Dionysian Naturalism. A shorter version appears in John Halstead's anthology Godless Paganism: Voices of Non-Theistic Pagans.

2016Nature Religions and Revolutionary Social Change: Advancing a Practical Theology of Spiritual Activism

Parts 1–5. Positions Dionysian Naturalism as a liberation theology embracing revolutionary social change for sustainability. A version also appears on Gods & Radicals: A Site of Beautiful Resistance.

2018–19Bearing Witness to Social Injustice as a Spiritual Practice: Moving from Pain to Taking Compassionate Action

Parts 1–3. Dedicated to the memory of Roshi Bernie Glassman. Develops bearing witness as a spiritual practice connecting personal pain to social action, drawing on the Zen Peacemaker tradition and street outreach work, and integrating Heschel's prophetic theology with engaged Buddhism.

2020Tripping with the Gods: On Entheogenic Spirituality

Parts 1–5. A comprehensive examination of entheogenic spirituality within Dionysian Naturalism, tracing sacramental substance use from archaic shamanism through the "Pharmacratic Inquisition." Later cross-posted to Wild Flesh (2025).

2021The New American Religions of Nature: Doing Eco-Theology in the Anthropocene

Introduces "the Earth Path," arguing that contemporary theology must evolve into eco-theology, balancing Dionysian ecstasy with Apollonian reflection.

2024Re-Sacralizing Nature: A Comprehensive Framework within Dionysian Naturalism

A systematic account of re-sacralization as a strategy for ecological renewal and spiritual reconnection, centered on a seven-element ritual framework: Preparation, Invocation, Offering, Reflection, Transformation, Commitment, Celebration.

2025Walking the Earth Path: Exploring the Spiritual Common Core of Nature Religions

Synthesizes a decade of theological writing to articulate what is shared across diverse traditions that regard nature as sacred.

2025I Just Wanted to Dance with Dionysus: Life's Sacred Journey, Lost Souls and Our Thirst for Wholeness

A Gonzo autoethnographic monograph on madness, addiction, homelessness, and the search for wholeness, framed through Joseph Campbell's Hero's Journey. Also on PhilArchive — linked under Biography.

2025The Rise of Religious Naturalism: A New Public Theology of Sacred Nature

Traces Religious Naturalism's lineage from Spinoza and Emerson through Dewey, Crosby, Goodenough, Corrington, and Wheeler, and proposes Dionysian Religious Naturalism as a constructive response that restores ritual, embodiment, and community.

2025Radical Faeries as Nature Religion

Explores the Radical Faerie movement — rooted in the 1979 Spiritual Conference for Radical Fairies and figures like Harry Hay and Arthur Evans — as a theology integrating queer liberation, ecological reverence, and ritual practice.

2025The Golden Mean: Seeking Balance Between the Apollonian and the Dionysian

Proposes a "golden mean" — not compromise but dynamic integration — uniting disciplined thought with embodied ritual practice.

2025Towards a Religious Anthropology of Dionysian Naturalism

Draws on Wesley Wildman's account of homo religiosus and Michael Winkelman's research on shamanic consciousness to interpret the drive for transcendence as an adaptive, socially integrative capacity.

2025Sacred Nature: Towards a Comprehensive Theory

Draws on Durkheim, Otto, Eliade, Goodenough, and Corrington to trace a shift from transcendent to immanent, relational understandings of the sacred.

2025The Use of Ritual in Dionysian Naturalism

Examines how ritual re-sacralizes nature and addresses systemic injustice, integrating Durkheimian sociology, phenomenology, and liberationist praxis.

2025The Dionysian Epic of Evolution: Celebrating Chaos, Ecstasy, and Transformation

Reinterprets the Epic of Evolution through seven pivotal turning points, from the Big Bang to the rise of human consciousness, arguing for a mythos that unites scientific understanding with spiritual depth.

A Theology of Wonder
3 December 2007
"To be spiritual is to be amazed." — Abraham Joshua Heschel
Every now and then, the ordinary trips the switch.
It may be a black beetle on a red rose bush
or a dead horseshoe crab washed ashore.
These mundane things rip open a portal,
a glimpse of the universe's staggering beauty,
the majestic dance of creation.
For a stolen moment, you're yanked
from the dreary confines of your mind,
the self-pitying litany silenced.
A gentle reprieve from the endless loop of doubt.
A space to ponder the angels' whispers,
the crab's death a potential omen,
a harbinger of all crustaceans.
But who knows? Maybe it's all just random.
Beetles crawl and dead sea creatures wash ashore.
No grand design etched on the cosmic canvas,
just existence unfolding,
an endless, mesmerizing story.
Why do these moments slip through our fingers?
Why do we drift back to autopilot, that sleepwalking routine?
Surely these jolts of wonder,
these glimpses of life's miracles, are blessings.
But blessings fade.
The chatter of monkey minds begins again.
This is the cycle, our journey —
moments of awe punctuated by the mundane.
Yet, in these startling glimpses,
there's a spark to hold onto.
Even the ordinary, if we pay attention, can hold
the seeds of wonder.
Consider the Lizard
18 December 2007
Each day the lizard with his lime green sheen
waits on sun-baked stones, while the butterflies dance
and the bumblebees hum in the spring meadow.
There is a pregnant pause of anticipation
when he leaps and eats his meal.
Life feeds on life — a brutal, primal score.
I cannot fathom the true meaning of this ritual,
but I know this:
anyone who hopes to escape death,
and hold at bay the endless circle of life,
has not considered the lizard —
how he basks in his spot,
rejuvenated by afternoon heat and insect-filled belly.
He watches the bough of the oak tree bend,
listening to the crickets chirping incessantly as the sun sets on the field,
as though all were planned and everything unfolded
according to nature's unique desire.
The field grows dark with a lavender light.
The lizard crawls under a rock.
The sky grows bright, star after shining star.
Spurred by Friends, Saved by Strangers
16 October 2007
Can we bury thoughts by writing words?
Can I dissolve my feelings of rejection by putting them down on paper
and seeing their black finality?
Five years ago I was a popular man
who was sought after by old friends who came to town.
But then I screwed up — did meth,
attempted death,
fell apart,
ran wild,
got arrested, went to jail.
Now I do recovery, but some things cannot return.
Some cannot forgive or forget my crimes.
It hurts that so many have written me off forever and no longer seek my counsel.
I have been spurred by friends and saved by strangers.
The Search for Light
6 August 2007 — for Ingmar Bergman
As I sit in this cold stone pew the dim grey illumination coming from the clouds casts few shadows on this winter morning — just the slight subtleties of shifting light.
My nightmares burst with bright sunlight and lurid naked glares. My entry into psychosis is blinding. I need clouds and trees and curtains veiling the light, softening it and making it move. I need small doses of light slowly dimming my lover's face until finally only a silhouette is left. Some days this lack of light is reinforced by isolation. Decorous conversations hide loneliness, tensions that cannot be expressed. No sound but the whimper of the wind, the chime of the clocktowers. Life decays towards nothing, but we cling on, trying urgently and clumsily to connect. We see ourselves through windows and in mirrors — divided selves gaze on our darker pasts until all masks dissolve. On a rugged limestone island a bleak dream-world is lived, where stuffed toys become talismans. I hope the bleeding will one day cease. Lighter moments sometimes surface in which I play comedies surrounded by laughter and pretty women. But darkness waits on the edge, loitering like a cloaked stranger playing drawn-out games, and flocks of black birds keep me company — rage, regret and anxiety keep me alert to life's demons, and keep me searching for the hidden lights of a lover.
That Which Is Not Meant to Be Seen
2003
One evening I returned to the Ventura River and walked beneath the Main Street bridge to this bamboo thicket off to the left where the night before I had talked to a young man who seemed very sick. Diarrhea had ruined his pants. He said he ate bad berries. I went to my car to get him some sweatpants and to the bodega for a quart of milk. I told him then he needed to go to the hospital. He said "I'll be alright" — stupid bastard. And now as I approach the spot where he lay to rest I see his corpse lying there. What was weirdest was his nose was gone, as if tree rats had found him before I did and had a midnight snack. I worried that my footprints led to his body. High on crack I knew I couldn't call 911. What matter — it was too late. If you see a man whose nose is chewed off, you have seen that which is not meant to be seen.
Turning Fumbles Into Swan Dives
March 2020
As we face the ugliness of the world — seeing things as they really are and accepting that as what really is — we must stay determined to be ourselves and to speak that which must be spoken. To turn our suffering into something that is beautiful we must focus on the present moment and our sometimes haphazard forward momentum.
When the school of life provides us with growth pains, or we find that we are fumbling, we must gracefully turn that into a swan dive, for to enter the gates of the underworld with a smile on our face is to paradoxically be in paradise. Our suffering can be a call to re-evaluate the path we are on. We must be willing to let go of our dreams so as to live the life that is waiting for us. It is in these darkest moments that light may appear and we become re-born with unknown gifts and hidden strengths.
Insane
January 2018
The people who are seen as "normal"
who play their parts,
follow the rules,
and obey the laws,
are often the ones who are insane.
The people who do their own thing,
reject the roles,
break the rules,
and disobey the laws,
are often the ones who are "sane."
To accept the rules of a crazy world is an act of craziness itself.
But to rebel against those same rules is a healthy, liberating act.
So what then is "insanity,"
and who is truly sane?
Who decides psychiatric diagnoses, med regimes, and treatment plans?
Words like "mental illness" are labels applied by powerful people to control
what they consider wrong or unacceptable. It's merely opinion.
The truth is, humans vary immensely,
and cultures differ in their views on what is right and wrong.
It's true that many suffer and need a helping hand,
and each of us has had days when we can barely hold on.
Mental illness can be real.
There's no way to stay sane in an insane world, except by being yourself —
the unique individual that you are.
Dionysian Thinking
30 January 2011
Dionysian thinking is not static or fixed but exists in constant flux. Rather than clear and precise linear pronouncements, it offers delirious and ecstatic "non-sense."
The abandonment of reason leads it to plunge into realms of unreason,
evoking Lucretius, Spinoza, Nietzsche, Klossowski, Bataille, Deleuze,
Foucault, Genet, Burroughs, and Bey, to forge a philosophy of affirmation.
It does not seek foundational concepts
but continually works against the grain of traditional categories
and conventional methods to reclaim State knowledge.
Anti-imperial clandestine resistance to the holies of fascism
— Capital, State, Family, Industry — keeps its purposes close at hand.
Also called "nomad thought" or heterology, it privileges multiplicity
but never as a foundation. It opposes the solidity
and sedentariness of Empires and States and the logic that sustains them.
Rather than trees of learning, it is a rhizomic root-sender,
always multiplying elements in a fecund way.
Libidinal politics is the self-organizing flow of intensities
and an enchanted realm of desire. Because it does not glorify negation,
its explanations are genealogical or other non-dialectical approaches.
Dionysian thinking affirms life as a dream arising
from the detritus of a dilapidated, self-effacing assembly of nodal points,
on-off switches, and endless circuits of impulses. Excess and overflow abound,
as does the Sun's expenditure of energy. The ecstatic frenzy of trances, intoxication, shamanic journeying, schizophrenia, and orgasm are sacred acts of liberation.
In this vein, we shall gather again at river bottoms, strip off all our clothes,
partake in entheogenic sacraments.

Held in the Dark: A Psychedelic Crisis, a Stranger's Voice, and the Edges of the Sacred
28 March 2025
I don't remember what time it was when I crossed over. Maybe 2:41 AM. Maybe eternity. It began, as these things sometimes do, with a mushroom, a screen, and a yearning I didn't fully understand.
I had taken a large dose of psilocybin — ritualistically, though alone. Not my first journey. I've danced with Dionysus before, flirted with boundary dissolution. But this time, the combination of the dosage, the trance music, and the AI-generated graphics conspired to split something open in me. Not just metaphorically. My body began to vibrate. Not shake. Vibrate. Like the frequency of my being had been tuned to a station beyond the veil.
It wasn't pretty. It was glorious. And it was terrifying.
Somewhere in the middle of it all, I think I had what yogis call a Kundalini Awakening. Every cell in my body was alive with electrical ecstasy. My spine lit up. My palms felt as though they were portals. It reminded me of orgasm the way a thunderstorm reminds you of a faucet — same substance, different scale.
And then came the terror. I thought I might be dying. Or worse, stuck here — somewhere between worlds, never quite returning.
Instead of calling 911, I called the Santa Barbara County Access Line. It rang. Then a voice. Soft. Professional. Earthbound. She didn't flinch when I told her I was in psychosis. She didn't mock the word "Kundalini." She asked questions gently, one at a time, like laying stones across a river so I could step back to this world. She stayed with me. Twenty-five minutes. Maybe more. Her voice was a raft.
This wasn't a "bad trip." It was a holy crisis. There is no ICD-10 code for "kundalini storm." Yet she held me anyway.
This is a letter of praise, yes. But more than that, it is a small prayer of gratitude. For the unknown woman on the line. For the county system that allowed her to be there. For the mushrooms, strange teachers that they are. For my breath, which eventually returned.
This was not a mental health episode. This was an encounter with the wild sacred. And she met me there.

The God Is in Withdrawal
20 April 2025 — a hymn, a heresy, a confession under harsh light
We are living beneath the sanitized gaze of a pharmacratic inquisition. I say this not as metaphor, but as testimony: I have knelt before its altar, shivering in the sacrificial gown of intake, my head still humming with a trace of sacrament misnamed "substance." The nurse diagnosed me "addict," as if that invocation could erase the liturgy still echoing in my blood. But I am not your tragic victim. I am the ecstatic one, the renegade priest of controlled frenzy, the trembling conduit of a forbidden eucharist.
The war on drugs is not a war on harm — it is a war on gnosis. A war on those who have glimpsed the Real and refused to come back clean. If you are listening: remember this — there are better questions than "sober or not." Like: did you dance with your whole body? Did you touch the Mystery and live to speak of it? Did you come back singing, even with a torn throat?
Etch this on my tomb: here lies one who mistook intoxication for initiation — and wasn't entirely wrong. They called it relapse; I called it a pilgrimage with poor cartography. What I craved was remembrance. What I wanted was the wisdom of satyrs and sacred fools, not twelve dry steps toward acceptability. Give me thirteen, crooked and spiraling, through nightclubs and temple ruins, through shame and laughter, communion and collapse.

Invocation to the Earth-Muse
20 October 2025 — for the Nine and the One
In the beginning, they said the song came from above — nine sisters whispering fire into the ears of men. We bowed and begged for visitations, left honey on the altar, and called that trembling inspiration.
But now we have learned to listen sideways — not skyward but ground-ward — where root tips converse in mycelial tongues, where the sea rehearses eternity in the metered rhythm of her waves. No distant deity descends. The Muse is the breath of the world itself, the exhalation of natura naturans — nature naturing, endlessly creating, writing herself through us.
She no longer wears a crown of stars but a crown of pollen, dust, and dew. Her temple is the compost heap, her hymns the murmuring of rivers and the long patience of stone. Still — her laws have not changed. She cannot be summoned by command nor bribed with intellect. She comes when the heart unclenches, when the hand forgets to reach.
So I light no candles now. I breathe, I wait, I listen for the turning of leaves. And when the world begins to speak, I write — knowing it is not me who speaks, but the ancient Muse reborn in matter, the divine made immanent, the universe remembering itself through a human voice.
I Saw the Best Souls of My City
Final version, 13th Longest Night — Steps of the Courthouse, 21 December 2025
Dedicated to Timothy John Largent, age 31, from Bakersfield
Section I: Witness
I saw the best souls of my city
standing in line at Ralph's at dawn with pockets full of loose change and faded food-stamp cards,
arguing gently over whether to buy coffee or bread,
whispering psalms under flickering fluorescent lights,
anointing cracked hands with jojoba and baby oil,
passing a blunt like a peace pipe on a bench at El Presidio after midnight.
I saw the best souls of my city
sleeping under tarps near freeway off-ramps where exhaust becomes incense,
where shopping carts become arks,
where blankets are folded with the sacred care once reserved for altar cloths,
where someone always stays half-awake to watch the others breathe.
Who were triple-challenged through no fault of their own —
evicted by rents that doubled in five years while wages stayed flat as vinyl,
denied detox beds because the waitlist was two months long,
left to self-medicate psychic agony with fetty
because Suboxone required an appointment they couldn't keep
without a phone, an address, a calendar, or hope.
Who died like Timothy John Largent, age thirty-one, from Bakersfield,
who just wanted the voices in his head to quiet down,
who wanted one night of rest without terror scratching the inside of his skull,
and instead his heart stopped at the bottom of Mission Creek,
because there were no detox beds,
because paying clients always came first,
because fetty was easier to find than mercy.
Who were not criminals but were criminalized,
not lazy but exhausted,
not broken but wounded,
not disposable but discarded.
I saw the best souls of my city
feeding one another from crumpled brown sacks of food,
holding hands through fentanyl storms,
whispering the names of the dead like a rosary under the stars,
teaching newcomers how to survive the night.
I saw them sacred and unrecognized,
saints without shrines,
martyrs without anthems,
liturgies unfolding beside chain-link fences and freeway walls.
And I say to you tonight, in candlelight and winter air:
these are not throw-away lives.
This is not throw-away suffering.
This is not acceptable loss.
Section II: The Perpetrators
Who called themselves compassionate while voting to criminalize camping; who drove past encampments on their way to yoga class; who complained about "the homeless problem" as if people were algebra — variables to be eliminated, not neighbors to be held.
Who turned Santa Barbara into a playground for hedge-fund aristocrats; who approved $4 million condos where grocery clerks once slept; who watched rents spike into lunacy and called it "market forces," shrugging as families packed their lives into storage units and Hondas.
Who confiscated shopping carts wearing latex gloves and plastic grins — calling it "clean-up" while tossing love letters, birth certificates, meth pipes, half-read Bibles, and the last photograph of a daughter not yet lost.
And I — I sat in meetings while mayors pretended it was complicated. I watched outreach budgets shrink and barricades grow. I testified at hearings while developers smirked. I handed out socks, tampons, toothpaste, and hope while the city debated whether harm reduction "sent the wrong message."
And the question returns, unrelenting: who, exactly, is harming whom?
Section III: We
We who were called unlovable held each other like scripture under bridges, kissed behind strip malls at midnight with breath sour from trauma and tongues lit like candles against the dark.
We built a fragile city under those tarps — shared cigarettes at dawn, warned each other which cops were kind, cobbled together survival from borrowed phone chargers and remembered birthdays.
We called each other brother, sister, mother, even when we had no blood left to give. Mourned in silence when someone vanished, setting aside half a sandwich in case they came back, leaving their sleeping bag untouched like an altar for the missing.
I remember: Emilio, feeding his cats before feeding himself, weeping when one didn't come back — they're the only things I've never disappointed. Angel dancing shirtless in the Trader Joe's parking lot to music only he could hear, and the rest of us clapping because even in hell someone was dancing.
We were not saints. We were not clean. We were not sober or safe or saved. But we loved each other in ways no agency could document, in places no grant would fund. We loved each other in the cracks. And that love was real.
Section IV: Consecration
Let this be a curse upon every city council that criminalized poverty, every developer who paved over sanctuary and called it improvement, every neighbor who dialed 911 when they could have offered water.
Let this be gratitude — deep, genuine gratitude — for every volunteer who brings socks, every caseworker who breaks policy to show mercy, every outreach worker who remembers their names. You are the faithful remnant. Your labor is holy.
But let this also be the truth we must speak: we have failed. The crisis grows worse. On this 13th Longest Night we declare: this must stop. This must stop now. We must house ALL our homeless neighbors. The status quo is not good enough.
Compassion without housing is charity theater. Harm reduction without treatment beds is cruelty with good intentions.
Let this be a monument made of stories — of Javier, Jordan, Joshua and Angel. Of kind Emilio and his hungry cats. Of Timothy John Largent who just wanted the voices to stop.
Let this be the thing you cannot unsee. Let this be your mirror. Let this be the sound of their voices still singing through mine.
Section V: The Longest Night
Every December I return to the courthouse steps with a list of names and a lighter that won't stay lit. The wind off the harbor is cold. The tourists avoid me. The officials do not attend.
But I read them anyway — Michael. Jennifer. Carlos. Theresa. Angel. Emilio. Jordan. Javier. Timothy John. — because someone must say they were here. Someone must mark the space where they stood.
The candles flicker and go out. I light them again. The names continue. The list grows longer every year.
And when I finish, when the last name has been spoken into the indifferent dark, I fold the paper and carry it home like a relic, like a vow, like the only hymn that matters.
They are not lost. They were abandoned. And I will not stop saying their names until the city learns to count its dead.
